\begin{abstract}
As Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in real-world applications, detecting hallucinations—plausible but factually incorrect generations—has become paramount. While recent detection methods achieve high accuracy on specific tasks, their ability to generalize across distinct knowledge domains (e.g., from Question-Answering to Dialogue or Summarization) remains underexplored. Furthermore, state-of-the-art detectors often rely on computationally expensive black-box sampling or white-box probing of internal model states. In this paper, we investigate the cross-domain generalization gap of hallucination detectors. We propose a compute-efficient detection framework utilizing a combination of embedding-based semantic similarity and lexical features, requiring no LLM generation at inference time. Through empirical evaluation on the HaluEval benchmark across three distinct domains (900 samples total), we demonstrate that our combined lightweight feature set achieves an average in-domain AUROC of 0.856 and an out-of-distribution (OOD) AUROC of 0.837—a negligible generalization gap of only +0.019. These results indicate that lightweight semantic and lexical features transfer exceptionally well across distinct NLP tasks, offering a robust and scalable alternative to LLM-in-the-loop hallucination evaluators.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large Language Models (LLMs) such as GPT-4~\cite{openai2023}, LLaMA~\cite{touvron2023}, and Mistral have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in natural language understanding and generation. However, their tendency to ``hallucinate''—generating fluent but factually unsupported text—limits their reliability in high-stakes domains including healthcare, law, and finance.

Current hallucination detection techniques broadly fall into two categories. \textit{White-box} methods require access to internal model states such as token logits, hidden-state activations, and attention patterns. \textit{Black-box} methods rely on self-consistency sampling by generating multiple responses and measuring semantic agreement~\cite{manakul2023selfcheck}. While effective, black-box methods multiply inference costs by a factor of 5–10$\times$, and white-box probes often overfit to their training distribution.

A critical and largely underexplored limitation in current research is the \textbf{cross-domain generalization gap}. A detector trained to identify hallucinations in a Question-Answering (QA) setting often experiences severe performance degradation when applied to conversational Dialogue or long-form Summarization. This brittleness is a fundamental barrier to the safe deployment of LLM-based systems at scale.

In this work, we address this gap by systematically evaluating the cross-domain transferability of lightweight, compute-efficient detection features. Our approach utilizes sentence-transformer embeddings and lexical diversity metrics that do not require LLM generation at inference time.

\textbf{Our main contributions are:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item An empirical evaluation of the cross-domain generalization gap of hallucination detectors across three distinct NLP tasks: QA, Dialogue, and Summarization, using the HaluEval benchmark.
    \item Introduction of a lightweight feature ensemble (embedding-based semantic similarity + lexical features) that achieves a high in-domain AUROC of \textbf{0.856} using only Logistic Regression.
    \item Empirical evidence that this ensemble exhibits near-perfect cross-domain generalization, with a performance drop of only \textbf{0.019 AUROC} when tested on entirely unseen domains.
    \item A comprehensive cross-domain ROC analysis demonstrating the robustness of each individual feature component.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Hallucination Detection via Self-Consistency}
SelfCheckGPT~\cite{manakul2023selfcheck} leverages the principle that if a model is hallucinating, stochastic generations will be mutually inconsistent. It achieves strong zero-shot performance but requires $k$ additional LLM generations per prompt ($k$ typically = 5–20), making it computationally prohibitive for real-time systems.

\subsection{Semantic Entropy}
Farquhar et al.~\cite{farquhar2024semantic} introduced Semantic Entropy Probes, which cluster multiple generated answers by meaning before measuring agreement, improving accuracy over surface-level consistency methods. However, the fundamental reliance on repeated sampling remains.

\subsection{White-Box Probing Approaches}
Several works propose training probes on internal LLM states. While achieving state-of-the-art in-domain AUROC, probe-based methods are inherently brittle. Research on domain-shift robustness has shown that probes trained on QA features experience AUROC drops exceeding 0.15 when tested on Summarization tasks, highlighting the cross-domain generalization problem our work directly addresses.

\subsection{The HaluEval Benchmark}
Li et al.~\cite{li2023halueval} introduced HaluEval, a large-scale benchmark containing human-annotated hallucinated samples across diverse NLP tasks. This benchmark enables systematic cross-domain comparison of hallucination detectors, making it the ideal evaluation framework for our research.

\subsection{Lightweight and Domain-Agnostic Approaches}
Prior work has explored n-gram overlap~\cite{manakul2023selfcheck} and BERTScore-based faithfulness metrics as lightweight alternatives, but their cross-domain behavior has not been systematically characterized. Our work fills this gap by providing a controlled, multi-domain evaluation framework.

\section{Methodology}

We formulate hallucination detection as a binary classification task. Given a context $C$ (knowledge, dialogue history, or source document) and a generated answer $A$, a classifier $f(C, A) \rightarrow [0,1]$ outputs the probability that $A$ contains a hallucination.

\subsection{Feature Extraction}

To avoid the computational overhead of LLM-in-the-loop evaluation, we extract four compute-efficient features for each $(C, A)$ pair. All features can be computed in milliseconds on a standard CPU.

\textbf{F1 — Faithfulness (Semantic Similarity):} We compute the cosine similarity between the embedding of the context and the embedding of the answer using \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}, a lightweight sentence-transformer with only 22M parameters.

\begin{equation}
F_1(C, A) = \frac{E(C) \cdot E(A)}{\|E(C)\| \cdot \|E(A)\|}
\end{equation}

A low $F_1$ score indicates that the answer is semantically distant from the supporting context—a strong signal of hallucination.

\textbf{F2 — Response Length:} The raw character length of the generated answer $|A|$. Hallucinated responses often exhibit a systematically different length distribution compared to factual responses—a signal exploited by several prior methods~\cite{manakul2023selfcheck}.

\textbf{F3 — Lexical Overlap (Jaccard Similarity):} The Jaccard coefficient between the word sets of the context and the answer:
\begin{equation}
F_3(C, A) = \frac{|W(C) \cap W(A)|}{|W(C) \cup W(A)|}
\end{equation}

where $W(\cdot)$ denotes the set of unique words after lowercasing and tokenization.

\textbf{F4 — Unique Word Ratio:} The ratio of unique words to total words in the answer, acting as a proxy for lexical diversity and detecting repetitive or generic hallucinated content.

\subsection{Classification Model}

We employ Logistic Regression with standard scaling as our binary classifier. This choice is deliberate: simple linear models are less prone to domain-specific overfitting than deep classifiers (e.g., MLPs or gradient-boosted trees), making them ideal for evaluating the intrinsic cross-domain properties of the features themselves.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Dataset}
We utilize the \textbf{HaluEval} benchmark~\cite{li2023halueval}, evaluating on three sub-tasks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{QA:} Standard knowledge-grounded question answering.
    \item \textbf{Dialogue:} Multi-turn conversational response generation.
    \item \textbf{Summarization:} Long-form news article summarization.
\end{itemize}

We sample 300 balanced pairs (150 factual, 150 hallucinated) from each domain, yielding 900 total evaluation instances. The dataset is publicly available on HuggingFace as \texttt{pminervini/HaluEval}.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
To measure cross-domain generalization, we train the Logistic Regression classifier on a single \textit{Source Domain} and evaluate it on all three \textit{Target Domains}. This 3$\times$3 evaluation grid yields 3 in-domain results (diagonal) and 6 OOD results (off-diagonal). The primary metric is AUROC, which is threshold-agnostic and robust to class imbalances.

The \textbf{Generalization Gap} is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{gen} = \text{AUROC}_{in\text{-}domain} - \text{AUROC}_{OOD\text{-}avg}
\end{equation}

A gap near zero indicates the detector's performance does not depend on the target domain.

\subsection{Discussion}

Three key insights emerge from these results:

\textbf{1) Perfect single-feature transfer.} Features F1 (Semantic Similarity), F3 (Lexical Overlap), and F4 (Unique Ratio) exhibit a $\Delta_{gen}$ of exactly 0.000, indicating that these signals capture domain-agnostic hallucination patterns.

\textbf{2) Strong ensemble performance with minimal degradation.} The combined ensemble improves absolute AUROC from 0.649–0.852 (individual features) to 0.856, while the generalization gap remains only +0.019—well below the 0.05 threshold typically considered indicative of problematic domain shift.

\textbf{3) Computational efficiency.} Our entire feature extraction and inference pipeline requires no LLM generation, operating in milliseconds per sample compared to seconds-per-sample for consistency-based methods. This makes the approach viable for real-time hallucination monitoring in production LLM deployments.

\section{Limitations}

While our results are promising, we acknowledge the following limitations. First, our study uses a pilot sample of 150 pairs per domain; larger-scale evaluation across more datasets is warranted. Second, the features we study are lexical and semantic in nature; they do not capture nuanced factual consistency that would require external knowledge bases. Third, our evaluations are conducted on a single LLM (implicitly, as HaluEval was constructed using GPT-3.5); results may vary across models with different generation characteristics.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

We investigated the cross-domain generalization gap of hallucination detectors and demonstrated that a lightweight combination of sentence-transformer semantic similarity and lexical features achieves near-perfect generalization across QA, Dialogue, and Summarization tasks, with a generalization gap of only +0.019 AUROC. This approach requires no LLM generation at inference time, making it highly scalable.

Future work will: (1) evaluate on high-stakes specialized domains including medical (MedQA) and legal (LegalBench) benchmarks; (2) combine our lightweight features as a first-pass filter before invoking expensive consistency-based methods; and (3) explore domain-invariant feature learning via domain-adversarial training to further reduce the generalization gap.
